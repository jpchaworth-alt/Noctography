Noctilucent cloud is ice at about 83 km, far above weather, lit by sunlight long after the ground is dark. It only forms when the summer mesosphere turns cold, which is why the season runs through your own summer and not the winter. There is no forecast for it anywhere: this flag tells you when and where they would be visible if they have formed, not that they have. Radar echoes and cold-mesosphere signals say something about the season, never about tonight.

A display that fades in the evening often comes back before dawn, as the cloud drifts back out of the Earth's shadow. Worth staying up for, or setting an alarm.

Real-world Bortle

The atlas tells you what a site reads on a perfect moonless night. This tells you what it reads tonight, with the moon, the haze and the town glow bouncing off thin cloud all counted.

It is measured at the zenith every 15 minutes and quoted as the median across the dark hours, so one bright half-hour does not define the night. A Bortle 3 site under a gibbous moon genuinely performs like a Bortle 5 one, and that is the number worth planning against.

An estimate, not a measurement. The geometry is straightforward: the aurora's green light comes from around 110 km up, so a band standing that far away still clears a horizon this far equatorward by a few degrees. What moves is everything feeding it. The edge of the oval for a given Kp wanders a couple of degrees, a substorm can drag it much further for twenty minutes, and the red light that mid latitudes see most of sits higher again. Trust it for "clear your horizon", not for "point the camera at 7°".

{{ aurRecLine }}

A watch means forecasters have seen a CME leave the sun and expect it here on that date. Arrival routinely slips six to twelve hours, so a watch shows on both nights it could touch. Magnitude stays unknown until the cloud reaches L1 and its field can be measured: arriving pointing north, the whole thing can fizzle with no aurora at all.

Recurrence is a different mechanism. Coronal holes sit still while the sun turns under them, so their streams come back about every 27 days and can be planned around. CME storms never recur. Recurrence is the weakest signal here and never ranks a night on its own.
Why Kp sits at the bottom of this page

It is a three-hourly planetary average, good for picking a night and useless for the next ten minutes. Solar wind, aurora probability, storm watches and the 27-day outlook all come from NOAA SWPC, sampled on your phone.

Where these numbers come from

The headline figure is meteors an hour you could expect to see. One relation is evaluated every 15 minutes through the night, separately for every active shower and the sporadic background.

rate = ZHR(t) × sin(h) × r^(lm − 6.5) × clear × 0.75

ZHR(t) is the shower's strength at this point in its cycle, anchored to solar longitude, with a narrow peak and a broad component for streams with long wings. sin(h) is the radiant altitude, tapered below 8° for extinction and terrain. r is the population index, an exponent, which is why one lost magnitude of sky darkness roughly halves the rate.

Limiting magnitude is built in linear brightness units: an airglow floor at 22.0, artificial ground light from the atlas, twilight, then moonlight from the Krisciunas & Schaefer (1991) model with Kasten & Young airmass, so a setting moon fades gradually. Thin cloud counts twice: it hides part of the sky and scatters town glow and moonlight back down, so the same 60% high cloud costs about a magnitude at Bortle 5. Humidity above 65% adds haze. Everything is scaled by 0.75, since ZHR assumes a perfect observer watching the whole sky at once.

Shower parameters are hand-encoded from the International Meteor Organization's calendar and working list. Cloud, humidity, temperature and dew point come from Open-Meteo, interpolated between hourly samples. Sky darkness comes from David Lorenz's 2025 light pollution atlas, baked into the app. Beyond 16 days the cloud figure is what this place typically does at that time of year, averaged over three years and a ±3 day window: useful for choosing a week.

Where these numbers came from
t = (35 × N + 30 × p) ÷ f

The NPF rule uses aperture and pixel pitch, which is why it lands so far from 500 ÷ focal length on a dense sensor. Both rules assume the worst case, a star on the celestial equator moving at the full 15° an hour.

In the framing view, where the app knows what you are pointing at, the figure is corrected for the fastest star actually in your frame. Near the pole that is worth a stop or more, for nothing. You never enter a declination.

It is a model of acceptable blur, not a law. Print big or pixel-peep and you will want the pin-sharp figure or less.

Data sources

Where every number comes from

Noctography has no server of its own. It reads these feeds directly from your device and does the astronomy in the browser. Credit where it is due.

Live feeds
Open-Meteo forecast open-meteo.com Cloud cover total, low, mid and high, relative humidity, temperature, dew point, wind speed and gusts. Hourly, 16 days ahead. Free for non-commercial use under CC BY 4.0. Open-Meteo historical weather (ERA5) archive-api.open-meteo.com Typical cloud beyond the 16 day forecast: three previous years, a three day window either side of the date. Describes a time of year, never a night. Open-Meteo geocoding geocoding-api.open-meteo.com Place search, so you can plan for somewhere other than where you are standing. NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center services.swpc.noaa.gov Planetary K index forecast, three-hourly, for tonight and the ten-night outlook. Public domain, like everything SWPC publishes. NOAA real-time solar wind, L1 services.swpc.noaa.gov/json/rtsw Interplanetary field strength and its north-south component (Bz) at one-minute cadence, plus wind speed, measured a million and a half kilometres upwind by the SOLAR-1 observatory with IMAP as backup. The history file is read once and then kept current from a sixty-byte summary, so the aurora page costs almost nothing to leave open. NOAA OVATION aurora model services.swpc.noaa.gov/json/ovation_aurora_latest.json A global grid of visible-aurora probability at one degree spacing, regenerated every five minutes from the live solar wind, looking 30 to 90 minutes ahead. The whole planet is fetched and then asked about your own coordinates on your device, so your position never leaves the phone. NOAA space weather alerts and watches services.swpc.noaa.gov/products/alerts.json Geomagnetic storm watches, warnings and alerts. Used for the G-level badge on a night when forecasters have seen a CME leave the sun and expect it to arrive. NOAA 27-day outlook services.swpc.noaa.gov/text/27-day-outlook.txt Predicted largest Kp for each of the next 27 days, updated weekly. The basis for the recurrence line: coronal holes come back round as the sun rotates.
CelesTrak: orbital elements celestrak.org Two-line elements for the International Space Station and for launches in the last thirty days. Dr T.S. Kelso's service, free for public use. The orbits themselves are propagated on your device, not fetched as a list of passes.
Baked into the app, no network
David Lorenz. World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness djlorenz.github.io The 2025 VIIRS-derived light pollution atlas, encoded into the app as two images. Your sky brightness, naked-eye limiting magnitude and Bortle class are read from it. International Meteor Organization imo.net Shower dates, solar longitude of maximum, ZHR and population index, hand-encoded from the IMO meteor shower calendar and working list. No outburst or dust-trail predictions.
Published models I lean on
Moonlight sky brightness model Krisciunas & Schaefer 1991, PASP How much a given moon, at a given altitude and distance from where you are looking, brightens the sky. The reason rates fade as the moon sets rather than snapping back. Airmass formula Kasten & Young 1989, Applied Optics Keeps the moonlight and extinction terms sane near the horizon, where a simple secant law breaks down. Google Fonts fonts.google.com Barlow, Cormorant Garamond and JetBrains Mono. Open source, SIL Open Font License.
Methodology, worked out here rather than fetched
Real-world Bortle Not a published figure. The Lorenz atlas value for your site, plus moonlight (Krisciunas & Schaefer), haze from Open-Meteo humidity, and town glow scattered back down by Open-Meteo cloud: evaluated at the zenith through the dark hours and converted back to a Bortle class. Shown so a dark site under a bright moon is not mistaken for a dark night.
Satellite passes SGP4, the standard orbit model, run in the browser against CelesTrak elements. A pass counts only when the satellite is in sunlight while you are not, and brightness comes from the usual range-and-phase-angle magnitude model. Only passes worth going outside for are named: the station has to reach magnitude −1 or better and climb above 20°, a train has to hold magnitude 3.2 and 25°. Anything fainter gets lost in the sky it crosses, so it is left out rather than listed. Three stars means bright and high, judged on each object's own scale. Starlink is only reported for launches inside the last eight days, while a batch is still strung out in a line; after that they spread around the orbit and stop being a spectacle.
Mist and fog risk Radiation fog needs three things at once: air close to its dew point, almost no wind to mix it away, and a clear sky to radiate heat to, which is exactly the night you would otherwise drive out for. Temperature, dew point, wind and forecast visibility come from Open-Meteo; the warning fires when all three line up, and defers to the visibility forecast where one exists.
Eclipses Worked out from the same sun and moon positions as everything else: a lunar eclipse when the moon enters the Earth's shadow cone, a solar one when the moon's disc crosses the sun as seen from your position on the globe rather than from its centre, which is what makes a solar eclipse local. Enough to tell you one is coming and roughly when. Times are to the nearest few minutes and the covered fraction is approximate, so check a dedicated prediction before planning a trip around one.
Looking ahead Ten nights ranked on clear sky first, then real-world darkness, then how much is going on overhead. Cloud is live forecast for about 16 days and climatology beyond it, shown faded when it is climatology. Aurora is only shown for nights the NOAA forecast actually reaches.
Real life light pollution map Not a published product. The Lorenz atlas is sampled on sixteen bearings out to 150 km, each sample weighted so a near town counts for more than a far one, and the whole ring is then scaled by tonight’s humidity and high cloud, because haze is what puts town light back in your eyes. Bar length and opacity carry the strength. The scaling factors are my own judgement, not anyone’s published model, and the map is a guide to which way to point rather than a photometric measurement.
Aurora geometry The forecast Kp is turned into the equatorward limit of the auroral oval using the standard published table, then converted to your own geomagnetic latitude with a dipole approximation of the 2025 pole. The band people photograph is placed 8 degrees of latitude poleward of that limit, and its height above your horizon comes from spherical geometry with green emission at about 110 km and red at 250. That 8 degree offset is mine, chosen so a Kp 6 night reads as a glow on the horizon rather than curtains overhead, which is what actually happens at these latitudes. It is the least settled number in the app and wants checking against real sighting records.
Camera against naked eye One signal, two thresholds. A sensor holding the shutter open picks up aurora roughly five to ten times fainter than a dark-adapted eye, which runs on colour-blind rod vision. The signal is the OVATION probability where you are and, more usefully, the best of the cells poleward of you that still clear your horizon. The thresholds start at about 12 and 42 percent and are then pushed up by moonlight, by cloud in that direction and by a brighter site, more steeply for the eye than the camera. Every one of those constants is a starting guess.
Bz trend A single Bz reading says little, so the pane reads the direction of travel: the mean of the last half hour, the fraction of the last hour spent below zero, and the length of the current run below minus two. South and holding needs a half-hour mean at or below minus four with three quarters of the hour below zero. Strength tiers are moderate, strong and severe at minus four, eight and fifteen. The dotted line on the trace marks what is arriving now, worked out from the distance to L1 divided by the measured wind speed. It is a warning, not a prediction: substorm timing cannot be forecast from L1 data however good it is.
Cloud in the direction that matters For aurora, cloud over your head is the wrong figure, so the forecast is also fetched for points 55 and 110 km poleward of you and averaged over the dark hours, with low cloud reported separately because low cloud is what shuts a horizon.

Sun, moon and radiant positions are computed on your device with no third party library. Nothing you enter, and no location, is sent anywhere except to the feeds above in order to fetch a forecast.
