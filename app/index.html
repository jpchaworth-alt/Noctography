Noctography

Noctography is a free planner for nightscape and landscape astrophotography. It answers two questions: is tonight worth going out for, and what should you point the camera at.

It needs JavaScript, because every calculation happens on your own device rather than on a server: sun and moon positions, the astronomical darkness window, real-world Bortle sky darkness, meteor rates, aurora thresholds, star trail geometry, NPF exposure limits and satellite passes.

The tools are Tonight, a fifteen-night forecast grid, Aurora, Meteors, Trails and timelapse planning, Sky with a framing view, a live AR overlay on the phone camera, a panorama calculator, and a saved kit profile.

Read what it does, and the common questions, on the Noctography home page.

Noctilucent cloud is ice at about 83 km, far above weather, lit by sunlight long after the ground is dark. It only forms when the summer mesosphere turns cold, which is why the season runs through your own summer and not the winter. There is no forecast for it anywhere: this flag tells you when and where they would be visible if they have formed, not that they have. Radar echoes and cold-mesosphere signals say something about the season, never about tonight.

A display that fades in the evening often comes back before dawn, as the cloud drifts back out of the Earth's shadow. Worth staying up for, or setting an alarm.

What is airglow

Airglow is the atmosphere making its own light, not reflecting anyone else’s. Green comes from oxygen in a thin layer around 95 km up, orange from hydroxyl just below it, and a deeper red from oxygen higher still. It is what the green and orange banding low in a wide nightscape usually is.

Its brightness follows the solar cycle, roughly 40% brighter around maximum. The number here is F10.7, the sun’s radio output at 10.7 cm, measured daily at Penticton in Canada since 1947 and used as the standard stand-in for the ultraviolet that drives the chemistry. Today’s reading is blended with the average over the last solar rotation, because oxygen up there builds over days rather than hours.

{{ glowMean }}

Why look low rather than up: the glowing layer is thin, so straight overhead you see through about 10 km of it, while at 15° above the horizon your line of sight travels obliquely through four times as much. That stacks the light up. Go much lower and the extra air in the way takes it back again, along with the town glow sitting on the horizon, which is why 10 to 20° is the sweet spot.

Treat the number as a fuel gauge for the spell you are in, not a forecast for tonight. Satellite averages track the flux closely; single nights at a single site are much looser. The ripples and bands people photograph come from gravity waves rolling through the upper atmosphere, thrown up by storms and terrain far below, and no public feed predicts those.

It is mostly a camera phenomenon: grey at best to the eye, colour only in long or stacked exposures. Low means faint, never absent. The callout only appears when the flux is high and the night can actually catch it: real darkness, little or no moon, a dark enough site and a reasonable chance of clear sky.

The cycle passed its peak in 2025, so High will get rarer between now and the next minimum around 2030. That is also why everyone’s 2024 skies looked so green.
Real-world Bortle

The atlas tells you what a site reads on a perfect moonless night. This tells you what it reads tonight, with the moon, the haze and the town glow bouncing off thin cloud all counted.

It is measured at the zenith every 15 minutes and quoted as the median across the dark hours, so one bright half-hour does not define the night. A Bortle 3 site under a gibbous moon genuinely performs like a Bortle 5 one, and that is the number worth planning against.

Two things worth knowing about the source. The atlas underneath is a satellite-derived model rather than a measurement, so it cannot know about the security light in the next field, and the fine grid covers north-west Europe: elsewhere the cells are coarse enough to average a small town away. If you own a sky quality meter, the manual override is the better answer.

Only high cloud is treated as scattering town glow back down at you. Mid and low cloud have already blocked the sky behind them, so they count as blocking alone: on a night of thick low cloud this figure will read dark, and it should be read alongside the cloud figure rather than on its own.

Two sources feed the line, a measurement up to now and a forecast beyond it, and the wind sits between them as a warning rather than a level.

Time up to and including now is from GFZ's half-hourly Hp30, a measurement of how disturbed the earth's magnetic field actually is. It has no ceiling, which is useful in a big storm where the older Kp scale simply stops at 9, and it is fresh enough every half hour to carry a trend worth reading.

GFZ will not release that file to a browser directly, so it arrives through a relay of my own. When it does, the reading is labelled Hp and marked measured. If the relay is unreachable, or the newest reading is more than 45 minutes old, the app falls back to NOAA's running estimate instead and labels it Kp and estimated. That fallback is averaged over three hours, so the level is still sound but the trend is much blunter, and the building and easing readings should be taken with more salt.

Hp30 steps in thirds, like Kp, so a genuine build arrives as a staircase rather than a smooth ramp. The direction is therefore read across the last two hours rather than off the last pair of readings, which means it is deliberately slow to call a change: better late than confidently wrong.

Spacecraft a million and a half kilometres sunward of us sample the wind about 40 minutes before it reaches here. The most useful number is Bz, the direction the wind's magnetic field points: pointing south, it latches onto ours and energy pours in. That reading is shown as a state, building or easing, rather than plotted as a level, because turning it into an index figure would mean a conversion I cannot source. It is the only genuine look into the future the app has, and it is deliberately not drawn as a measurement.

Beyond that we can't use anything directly measured, so the line hands over gradually to NOAA's Kp forecast, issued in three-hour blocks. Confidence decays rapidly over time. NOAA's own checking shows that forecast tends to over-call moderate nights (i.e. it tends to disappoint), which is why the far end is drawn as a wide band.

The chance itself is worked out for where you are standing or the location you've selected. It folds in how far the band would have to stretch to reach you, how high it would stand above your horizon, moonlight from the published sky-brightness model, the cloud forecast over that part of the sky, and the light pollution towards the poleward horizon rather than overhead.

The answer is always one of five words, in this order: unlikely, possible, very possible, likely, very likely. Nights after tonight stop at very possible, and anything from the 27-day outlook stops at possible, because past tonight the uncertainty is in the forecast rather than in the sky.

That word is not read off the middle of the forecast. It is averaged across the whole spread of levels the forecast allows, so the doubt is inside the figure rather than drawn beside it. Because the curve from level to chance bends over at the top, a confident-looking night comes down a little and a hopeless one comes up: the hedging is arithmetic, not editorial. It is also taken from the best continuous ninety minutes rather than the single best quarter of an hour, since the best of sixty noisy estimates runs high on its own and is not a period you could stand outside for anyway.

The chart plots the disturbance itself rather than the chance, with the levels you personally need drawn across it. Drawing the doubt on the chance meant drawing an uncertainty about a probability on an axis that was already a probability, which made the shading wide enough to contradict the words above it on almost any good night. On the index the shading is simply how far either side the forecast could land, and you can watch it cross your own thresholds.

The geometry is a tilted-dipole model of the magnetic pole and a standard oval-edge relation. Both are tuned for the UK and northern Europe and get looser the further you go from it, particularly in the southern hemisphere. The height of the arc is described in words rather than degrees, because the offset behind those degrees comes from my own observing rather than from a published archive.

It's important to note that this isn't a sophisticated aurora-chasing forecast. Hardcore chasers use their own methodology from the underlying data or use dedicated apps like Norlys. The purpose of this forecast is to give generalist nightscape photographers a heads up that their night might be about to get better.

Two things matter here, and it is how they sit together that tells you something.

The first is the wind arriving from the sun, measured by spacecraft out ahead of us. When its magnetic field points south it connects with ours and energy pours in. That is the push.

The second is what the earth is actually doing about it, measured by magnetic observatories on the ground and reported as one number for the whole planet. That is the result.

A hard push with the number climbing means it is happening and getting bigger. A hard push with the number flat means energy is going into the earth’s magnetic tail and has not come back out yet, which usually ends in a sudden brightening. A hard push with the number falling is the lull between two of those. Same two readings, three quite different pieces of advice.

Nothing here can time a brightening. They last twenty minutes to an hour and nobody forecasts them. The honest version is that the conditions for one are in place.

{{ aurRecLine }}

These come from NOAA's Kp forecast, three-hour blocks up to three days out, matched against each night's dark window rather than a rolling average around now. A night only reads as likely when the number, the geometry, the darkness and the cloud all agree.

A watch means forecasters have seen a coronal mass ejection, or CME, leave the sun and expect it here on that date. A CME is a billion tonnes of the sun’s atmosphere thrown outwards, and it takes one to three days to cross to us. Arrival routinely slips six to twelve hours, so a watch shows on both nights it could touch, and its size stays unknown until it reaches the spacecraft and its field can be measured. Arriving pointing north, the whole thing can fizzle.

Recurrence is a different mechanism. Coronal holes sit still while the sun turns under them, so their streams come back about every 27 days and can be planned around. CME storms never recur.

Everything here is a likelihood for where you are standing tonight, not a promise of aurora. Index, solar wind, hemispheric power and Kp forecast from NOAA SWPC, half-hourly Hp30 from GFZ where it can be reached, cloud from Open-Meteo, worked out on this device.

Where these numbers come from

The headline figure is meteors an hour you could expect to see. One relation is evaluated every 15 minutes through the night, separately for every active shower and the sporadic background.

rate = ZHR(t) × sin(h) × r^(lm − 6.5) × clear × 0.75

ZHR(t) is the shower's strength at this point in its cycle, anchored to solar longitude, with a narrow peak and a broad component for streams with long wings. sin(h) is the radiant altitude, tapered below 8° for extinction and terrain. r is the population index, an exponent, which is why one lost magnitude of sky darkness roughly halves the rate.

Limiting magnitude is built in linear brightness units: an airglow floor at 22.0, artificial ground light from the atlas, twilight, then moonlight from the Krisciunas & Schaefer (1991) model with Kasten & Young airmass, so a setting moon fades gradually. Thin cloud counts twice: it hides part of the sky and scatters town glow and moonlight back down, so the same 60% high cloud costs about a magnitude at Bortle 5. Humidity above 65% adds haze. Everything is scaled by 0.75, since ZHR assumes a perfect observer watching the whole sky at once.

Shower parameters are hand-encoded from the International Meteor Organization's calendar and working list. Cloud, humidity, temperature and dew point come from Open-Meteo, interpolated between hourly samples. Sky darkness comes from David Lorenz's 2025 light pollution atlas, baked into the app. Beyond 16 days the cloud figure is what this place typically does at that time of year, averaged over three years and a ±3 day window: useful for choosing a week.

Where these numbers came from
t = (35 × N + 30 × p) ÷ f

The NPF rule uses aperture and pixel pitch, which is why it lands so far from 500 ÷ focal length on a dense sensor. Both rules assume the worst case, a star on the celestial equator moving at the full 15° an hour.

In the framing view, where the app knows what you are pointing at, it uses the fastest star actually in your frame instead. Near the pole that is worth a stop or more, for nothing.

It is a model of acceptable blur, not a law. Print big or pixel-peep and you will want the pin-sharp figure or less.

Real-world Bortle The atlas value for your site, plus moonlight, haze from humidity and glow scattered back by cloud, at the zenith across the dark hours.
Your skyline The elevation data ray-cast out to 150 km with curvature and refraction, giving a real horizon, terrain rise and set times, and a shot window per subject.
Which satellite passes are named Only ones worth going outside for: the station at magnitude −1 and 20°, a Starlink train at 3.2 and 25°, and only within eight days of launch while the batch is still in a line.
High and low water Nobody publishes free UK tide predictions, so this works them out: a month of the gauge’s own record fitted to the ten tidal constituents a month of record can separate, then run forward, renewed monthly. Expect a few minutes and a few centimetres against a published table. Heights are in the gauge’s own datum, not chart datum. What no table predicts is surge: when the measured level and the prediction disagree, the difference is the weather. Coverage is the UK, Ireland, the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands; elsewhere the tile does not appear.
Mist and fog risk Fires when air near its dew point, near-still wind and a clear sky line up. Defers to the visibility forecast where there is one.
Eclipses From the same sun and moon positions as everything else, computed for your position rather than the centre of the Earth. Times to the nearest few minutes.
Looking ahead Ten nights ranked on clear sky, then real darkness, then what is overhead. Faded where cloud is climatology rather than forecast.
Light pollution around you The atlas sampled on sixteen bearings out to 150 km, near towns weighted more, then scaled by tonight’s humidity and high cloud. My own weightings, not a published model.
Aurora geometry Kp to the equatorward limit of the oval by the published table, then your geomagnetic latitude by dipole. The photographed band sits 8° poleward of the limit, which is my number and the least settled one here.
Camera against naked eye One OVATION signal, two thresholds, about 12% and 42%, pushed up by moonlight, cloud in that direction and a brighter site. Starting guesses.

Privacy

Noctography has no server of its own, no account and no sign-in. I do not know who you are and I have not built anything that could tell me.

Three things leave your device. Your coordinates go to the feeds above so they can return a forecast, a place name or the ground under you: there is no way to ask for the weather where you are standing without saying where that is. Map tiles are fetched as you pan, so Esri and OpenStreetMap see roughly which patch of ground you are looking at. And the app counts usage with GoatCounter: a page view, which tab you opened, whether you added it to your home screen, and whether AR got the camera going. GoatCounter sets no cookies, stores no IP address and cannot follow anyone between sites; it derives a country and a browser from the request and discards the rest. Counts are per visit, not per tap.

Nothing else goes anywhere. Your saved places, your skyline and the obstructions you have drawn, your kit profile, your colour scheme and everything you type stay in this browser’s own storage, on this device, and are never uploaded. The aurora probability grid is fetched whole and asked about your position here, so that one never leaves at all. The one-file copy of the app you can keep on a laptop reports nothing whatsoever.

The ethos, in one line: the app should know where you are only for as long as it takes to answer a question, and I should never know at all.
